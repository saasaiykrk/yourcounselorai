The Core Eight

The therapies every psychologist and counsellor should master

Written byYajuvendra Khajekar•14 October 2026•For psychologists and counsellors
About this framework. The “Core Eight” is the author’s proposed learning framework. It is a perspective for professional discussion, not an official or exhaustive ranking of therapies.

The problem with collecting therapies

There are now more than 400 named forms of psychotherapy. Every year a few more arrive, each with its own workshop, certificate and set of acronyms. Many young psychologists and counsellors respond the way any conscientious professional would: they try to learn as many as they can. Their profiles list ten or fifteen modalities. Their bookshelves are full.

Then a client walks in and the collection does not help.

Consider a counsellor I will call Meera, two years into practice, with certificates in NLP, art therapy, hypnotherapy, inner child work and a three-day CBT workshop. Her client, Rohan, is 28 and has panic attacks on the metro. Meera tries relaxation, then visualisation, then inner child work. Six months later Rohan still avoids the metro, now takes autorickshaws everywhere, and has started avoiding malls too. What he needed was one thing done properly: twelve sessions of CBT with structured exposure. A therapist who had mastered that single method would very likely have had him back on the metro within three months.

This is the central point of this article. Breadth does not make you effective. Depth in the right few methods does. Most problems that bring people to therapy are kept going by a small number of processes, and a small number of well-tested therapies target those processes directly. If you master eight of them, you will be able to help the large majority of the people who walk through your door. You will also know clearly when someone needs something you cannot offer, and where to send them.

The eight are:

  1. Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT)
  2. Exposure therapy
  3. Motivational Interviewing (MI)
  4. Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT) skills
  5. Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT)
  6. Interpersonal Psychotherapy (IPT)
  7. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)
  8. Behavioural parent training and one evidence-based couple therapy

The rest of this article explains why these eight, what each one actually does, what mastering it looks like, how they combine, and how to learn them over three years.

What actually makes therapy work

Before choosing methods, it helps to be clear about what produces change in the first place. Two bodies of evidence matter here.

The relationship carries a large share of the change

Across decades of research, a few factors predict good outcomes regardless of the model used: a strong working alliance, the client's hope and expectation that therapy will help, a credible explanation of the problem, and a structured plan both people believe in. The individual therapist also matters a great deal. Some therapists get consistently better results than others using the same method.

This does not mean the method is irrelevant. It means a method works best when it is delivered inside a warm, organised, collaborative relationship. A brilliant exposure plan handed to a client who does not trust you will not be carried out.

Most problems are kept going by a handful of processes

The second body of evidence is about maintenance. Diagnoses look very different on the surface, but underneath them the same few processes appear again and again. These are what keep a problem going long after whatever started it.

Process What it looks like in the room Where you see it
Avoidance"I just don't go there any more." Escape, rituals, reassurance-seeking, safety behavioursPanic, phobias, OCD, PTSD, social anxiety, health anxiety
Unhelpful thinking"I'm useless." "Something terrible will happen." Rumination and worryDepression, anxiety, low self-esteem, perfectionism
Ambivalence"I know I should stop, but..."Addictions, adherence, any client who is not sure about change
Emotional overwhelmEmotions that rise fast, go high and act before thinkingSelf-harm, borderline pattern, anger, impulsivity
Stuck beliefs after trauma"It was my fault." "No one can be trusted."PTSD, sexual assault, childhood abuse
Relationship strainConflict, loss, isolation, a role that has changedDepression, grief, perinatal problems, life transitions
Struggling against what cannot be changedFighting pain, illness or loss so hard that life shrinksChronic pain, chronic illness, caregiving, burnout
Patterns between peopleCoercive cycles between parent and child, or between partnersChild behaviour problems, couple distress

Each of the eight therapies in this article targets one or more of these processes directly. That is why so few methods can cover so much ground.

How the eight were chosen

Every therapy considered had to pass four tests.

  1. Strength of evidence. Multiple randomised trials and a place in major guidelines such as NICE, the WHO mhGAP programme and the APA's list of research-supported treatments.
  2. Breadth. It must work across several problems, not just one narrow diagnosis.
  3. Learnability. A trained psychologist or counsellor must be able to reach competence through a manual, a workshop and supervised cases, without years of personal analysis.
  4. Fit with Indian practice. It must work, or have been adapted to work, with joint families, varied languages, stigma, short session budgets and clients who expect some advice and structure.

Some excellent therapies did not make the core list because they fail the breadth or learnability test. Schema Therapy, EMDR and CBT for psychosis are examples. They are powerful, but they are best learned as a specialisation after the core is in place.

What the eight can and cannot do

When I mapped these eight against a matrix of 73 common adult presenting problems, one of them was the first-line psychological treatment for about 60. That is roughly four in every five problems an outpatient psychologist sees. Most of the rest need a psychiatrist, a specialist service or non-clinical help such as legal aid, alongside or instead of therapy. Section 7 covers those.

So the honest claim is this: the eight will not solve everything, and no set of therapies can. But mastered properly, used in combination and backed by good referral habits, they will let you help the large majority of your clients well.

The eight therapies

Each therapy below follows the same pattern: what it is, what it targets, where it works, what mastery looks like, the mistakes to avoid, and notes for practice in India. A short case shows it at work.

1. Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT)

What it is. CBT starts from a simple, testable idea: how we interpret events, and what we do in response, shapes how we feel. Change the interpretations and the behaviours, and the feelings follow. It is structured, time-limited (usually 8 to 20 sessions), collaborative and focused on the present. The client learns skills to use long after therapy ends.

What it targets. Unhelpful thinking, unhelpful behaviour (especially withdrawal and inactivity), and the cycles that link them.

Where it works. Depression, every anxiety disorder, insomnia (as CBT-I), health anxiety, chronic pain, eating disorders (as CBT-E), anger, low self-esteem, perfectionism and many life stressors. Most other evidence-based therapies are adaptations of CBT, so learning it well makes everything else easier.

Three tools inside CBT that you must master separately:

  • Behavioural activation. Scheduling activities that bring pleasure, achievement or meaning, step by step, especially in depression. In moderate to severe depression it is often more useful than working on thoughts, because a depressed mind cannot argue its way out of bed.
  • Problem-solving. A structured routine: define the problem, list options, weigh them, choose, act, review. Invaluable for clients overwhelmed by practical difficulties such as debt, job loss or a sick parent.
  • Behavioural experiments. Testing a belief in real life instead of debating it. "If I speak up in the meeting, everyone will think I'm stupid" becomes a prediction the client checks.

What mastery looks like.

  • You build a case formulation with every client: a shared map of how their problem started and, more importantly, what keeps it going. Every technique you use follows from this map.
  • You use guided discovery, asking questions that help the client reach conclusions themselves instead of telling them their thoughts are wrong.
  • Every session has an agenda, a review of the last homework, focused work, and new homework agreed together.
  • You measure progress (PHQ-9, GAD-7 or a problem-specific scale) and adjust when the numbers stall.

Common mistakes.

  • Handing out thought records without a formulation. Worksheets are not therapy.
  • Arguing with thoughts. If the client feels corrected, they stop telling you their real thoughts.
  • Skipping homework review. Clients quickly learn that homework does not matter.
  • Staying in "talking about" mode when the problem needs behaviour change.

Practice in India. Clients often expect guidance from an expert and may find questioning unfamiliar at first. Explain the model plainly, use local examples and proverbs, and frame homework as "practice" rather than "assignments". Involve family where the client wants it, especially for behavioural activation, where a supportive spouse or sibling can make a big difference. CBT has been successfully adapted and tested in India in Hindi, Marathi, Kannada and other languages, including in versions delivered by trained lay counsellors.

Example. Sunil, 45, a bank officer, has been low for eight months since being passed over for promotion. He has stopped walking in the evening, stopped visiting his brother and spends weekends in front of the television. His PHQ-9 is 17. The formulation shows a cycle: "I'm a failure" leads to withdrawal, withdrawal leads to fewer good experiences, which confirms "I'm a failure". The first four sessions are pure behavioural activation, starting with a 15-minute walk three times a week and one call to his brother. From session five, he starts noticing and testing his "failure" thoughts against evidence from his 20-year career. By session 12 his PHQ-9 is 6.

2. Exposure therapy

What it is. Exposure means helping a client approach what they fear, in a planned and graded way, without escaping or neutralising the fear. Technically it sits inside CBT, but it deserves its own place because it is the single most powerful technique for anxiety-based problems and the one therapists most often avoid. The main forms are in vivo exposure (real situations), interoceptive exposure (feared body sensations), imaginal exposure (memories or feared images) and Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) for OCD.

What it targets. Avoidance and safety behaviours. Every time a person avoids, escapes, checks, washes or seeks reassurance, the fear is protected from being tested. Exposure lets the brain learn that the feared outcome does not happen, or is bearable when it does.

Where it works. Panic disorder and agoraphobia, specific phobias, social anxiety, OCD, PTSD (as Prolonged Exposure), body dysmorphic disorder, health anxiety and nightmares (through imagery rehearsal).

What mastery looks like.

  • You build a hierarchy of feared situations with the client, rated from least to most distressing.
  • You design each exposure as a test of a specific prediction: "If I touch the door handle and don't wash, I will get seriously ill." After the exercise you ask: what happened, and what did you learn? Current research shows that learning comes from violating the expectation, not only from anxiety fading.
  • You identify and remove safety behaviours. Carrying a water bottle, sitting near the exit, asking a family member "Are you sure it's clean?" all undermine exposure.
  • You tolerate the client's distress. This is the hardest part. Exposure is uncomfortable by design, and a therapist who rescues the client too early teaches them the fear was justified.

Common mistakes.

  • Moving up the hierarchy too slowly, or staying on relaxation for weeks before starting.
  • Allowing reassurance to continue, from you or from family.
  • Doing exposure only in the session and never assigning daily practice.
  • Stopping an exercise at peak distress, which can strengthen the fear.

Practice in India. Family accommodation is very common: parents wash for the client, spouses check locks, relatives answer the same reassurance question 50 times a day. Bring family into at least one session and agree how they will step back. Distinguish culturally normal religious practice from compulsions. Prayer is not the problem; repeating a prayer 40 times because it "didn't feel right" is. Discuss exposures involving religious or purity themes respectfully, and if needed with the family's spiritual adviser.

Example. Fatima, 31, washes her hands 60 to 80 times a day and asks her husband several times every evening whether she touched anything dirty. Her Y-BOCS is 29. The hierarchy starts with touching the outside of her handbag and not washing for 30 minutes, and rises to cooking a meal after touching the floor. Her husband attends session three and agrees to answer reassurance questions only with "I know you can handle this uncertainty." After 16 sessions of ERP with daily practice, her Y-BOCS is 11.

3. Motivational Interviewing (MI)

What it is. MI is a collaborative way of talking that strengthens a person's own motivation to change. Developed by William Miller and Stephen Rollnick for problem drinking, it is now used across health care. It is less a set of techniques and more a style: partnership instead of authority, acceptance instead of judgement, compassion, and drawing out the person's own reasons for change instead of supplying them.

What it targets. Ambivalence. Almost every client is of two minds about change. Ambivalence is normal, but if the therapist pushes for change, the client naturally argues the other side, and ends up talking themselves out of it.

Where it works. Substance use, alcohol, tobacco, gambling and gaming. Also medication adherence, health behaviours, eating disorders, and any client who says "I don't know if I really need to be here." Used as a brief opening phase of two or three sessions, MI improves engagement and outcomes in CBT and other therapies.

What mastery looks like.

  • You are fluent in OARS: open questions, affirmations, reflections and summaries. Skilled MI practitioners make at least two reflections for every question.
  • You notice and draw out change talk, the client's own statements about desire, ability, reasons and need to change, and you reflect it back.
  • You resist the righting reflex, the urge to fix, warn and advise. When you feel it, you ask permission first: "Would it be all right if I shared some information about that?"
  • You move through the four processes in order: engaging, focusing, evoking, planning. You do not jump to a plan before the person is ready.

Common mistakes.

  • Using MI as a clever way to make clients do what you want. Clients sense it.
  • Asking question after question without reflecting.
  • Arguing with "sustain talk" (reasons not to change) instead of reflecting it and moving on.

Practice in India. Families, doctors and teachers here often deal with addiction through lectures, threats or shame. Clients arrive expecting more of the same. A therapist who listens without lecturing is often a new experience, and it builds trust quickly. MI also works well with family members: help a worried wife move from pleading to curious, non-confronting conversations with her husband. Lay-counsellor programmes in India, such as Counselling for Alcohol Problems, have shown that MI-based care works here.

Example. Prakash, 38, comes only because his wife threatened to leave over his drinking. "I'm not an alcoholic. Everyone in my office drinks." The therapist does not argue. "It sounds like drinking is a normal part of life at work, and coming here feels unfair." Prakash relaxes. Later: "What worries you, if anything, about how things are going?" He mentions his daughter asked why he shouts at night. The therapist reflects: "Your daughter matters a lot to you, and you don't like her seeing you that way." That is the first change talk. Three sessions later Prakash sets his own goal: no drinking on weeknights.

4. Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT) skills

What it is. DBT was developed by Marsha Linehan for people with chronic suicidal behaviour and what is now called the borderline pattern. Its central idea is a balance, or dialectic, between acceptance ("your feelings make sense given your history") and change ("and these behaviours are hurting you, so let's learn new ones"). Full DBT has four parts: individual therapy, a weekly skills group, phone coaching between sessions, and a consultation team for therapists. This article recommends that every clinician master the skills component. It is teachable, flexible and useful far beyond borderline presentations.

What it targets. Emotional overwhelm: emotions that rise fast, peak high, take long to settle, and drive impulsive action.

The four skill modules:

Module What the client learns Example skill
MindfulnessTo observe thoughts and feelings without being swept awayObserve, describe, participate, non-judgementally
Distress toleranceTo survive a crisis without making it worseSTOP (stop, take a step back, observe, proceed mindfully); paced breathing; distraction with ACCEPTS
Emotion regulationTo understand emotions and reduce vulnerability to themCheck the facts; opposite action; PLEASE (sleep, food, exercise, illness care)
Interpersonal effectivenessTo ask for what you need and say no while keeping relationships and self-respectDEAR MAN for making requests

Where it works. Self-harm, the borderline pattern, anger, impulsivity, emotional crises, binge eating and substance use driven by emotion. Skills-only groups are increasingly used for adolescents, families and mixed clinical groups.

What mastery looks like.

  • You validate first. You can find and name the part of a client's reaction that makes sense, even when the behaviour is harmful.
  • You run a chain analysis after every problem behaviour: the trigger, the vulnerabilities that day, the thoughts, feelings and urges, the behaviour, and the consequences. Then you find the links where a skill could have changed the outcome.
  • You teach skills experientially, with practice in the room and real homework, not as lectures.
  • You use a diary card to track urges, behaviours and skills use every week.

Common mistakes.

  • Teaching skills without validation. The client hears "you're doing it wrong" and drops out.
  • Treating skills-only work as a replacement for full DBT in people with frequent, high-risk self-harm. They need the full programme or a specialist referral.
  • Getting pulled into a crisis every session, so skills are never taught.

Practice in India. Strong emotion in young people, especially women, is often met at home with dismissal or criticism: "you are too sensitive", "adjust". DBT's biosocial explanation, a sensitive temperament meeting an environment that did not validate it, gives families a non-blaming way to understand their child. Run a parallel family skills session where possible. Validation by elders, even once, can be powerful.

Example. Ananya, 22, a college student, self-harms after arguments with her mother, about twice a week. A chain analysis shows a pattern: poor sleep, skipped meals, a critical comment, a flood of shame, then self-harm within minutes. She learns the STOP skill and paced breathing to ride out the first minutes of the urge, distraction skills for the minutes after, the PLEASE skills for sleep and meals, and DEAR MAN for one honest conversation with her mother. Her mother attends two family sessions. After four months, self-harm has stopped for six weeks and Ananya is using her diary card daily.

5. Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT)

What it is. CPT, developed by Patricia Resick, is a 12-session, manualised therapy for post-traumatic stress. Instead of focusing mainly on reliving the trauma, it focuses on the meanings the person has taken from it. Many people recover naturally after trauma. Those who don't are often stuck on beliefs such as "It was my fault", "I should have stopped it", or "The world is completely unsafe".

What it targets. Stuck points: beliefs about the trauma that keep the person stuck. CPT distinguishes two kinds. Assimilated beliefs distort the event to protect old beliefs ("If I had dressed differently, it wouldn't have happened"). Over-accommodated beliefs over-generalise from it ("No man can ever be trusted").

Where it works. PTSD after any trauma: assault, sexual violence, accidents, disasters, combat and childhood abuse. For complex trauma, CPT works well after a stabilisation phase, using DBT skills or STAIR.

How the 12 sessions run:

  1. Sessions 1 to 2: education about PTSD, and an impact statement in which the client writes why they think the trauma happened and how it changed their beliefs.
  2. Sessions 3 to 5: identifying stuck points and the link between events, thoughts and feelings, using ABC worksheets. Optionally, a written account of the trauma.
  3. Sessions 6 to 7: challenging stuck points with Socratic questions and challenging-beliefs worksheets.
  4. Sessions 8 to 12: five themes, one by one: safety, trust, power and control, esteem, intimacy. The client rewrites the impact statement at the end.

What mastery looks like.

  • You go after self-blame first, because assimilated stuck points block recovery the most.
  • You use Socratic dialogue patiently, never telling the client their belief is wrong, but asking questions until they see the event more accurately.
  • You do not avoid the index trauma. Therapists often unconsciously steer away from the worst event to protect the client, and the client stays stuck.
  • You track symptoms every session (for example with the International Trauma Questionnaire) and expect steady change.

Common mistakes.

  • Accepting "it was my fault" because the client feels strongly. Strong feelings are not evidence.
  • Turning sessions into supportive listening without worksheets.
  • Starting CPT while the person is still in danger, for example still living with an abusive partner. Safety comes first.

Practice in India. Shame and family honour can make self-blame especially strong after sexual violence, and families may pressure the person to stay silent. Be clear about mandatory reporting under the POCSO Act when a minor is involved, and about medico-legal support. CPT can be delivered without a written trauma account, which suits clients with limited literacy or strong privacy concerns.

Example. Kavita, 26, was assaulted by a colleague a year ago. She has nightmares, avoids her old office area and tells herself, "I should never have stayed late." Her impact statement is full of self-blame. Through Socratic questions ("Who decided to commit the assault?" "Did staying late cause it, or did he?"), she reaches a new belief: "I stayed late to finish work. He chose to hurt me." By session 12, her trauma symptoms have fallen below the clinical threshold and she has gone back to the gym she had stopped visiting.

6. Interpersonal Psychotherapy (IPT)

What it is. IPT, developed by Gerald Klerman and Myrna Weissman, is a time-limited therapy (usually 12 to 16 sessions) built on a plain observation: mood problems and relationship problems feed each other. IPT does not dig into the distant past or challenge thoughts. It links the client's current symptoms to one main interpersonal problem and works on that problem directly.

What it targets. Relationship strain, in one of four problem areas:

Problem area What it means Typical focus
GriefA death that has not been mournedMourning, and rebuilding relationships and activities
Role disputesA conflict with someone importantClarifying expectations, improving communication, negotiating
Role transitionsA major life change: marriage, a baby, retirement, migration, diagnosisMourning the old role, building skills and support for the new one
Interpersonal deficitsIsolation, few close relationshipsBuilding and deepening connections

Where it works. Depression (including in adolescents and older adults), perinatal depression, grief, life transitions and mood problems triggered by relationships. It is the best alternative when CBT doesn't fit a client, for example someone who finds worksheets alienating but is willing to talk about the people in their life.

What mastery looks like.

  • You do an interpersonal inventory in the first sessions: a careful review of every important relationship, what works, what doesn't and what has changed.
  • You give the client the sick role: depression is named as a treatable illness, which reduces self-blame and gives temporary permission to do less.
  • You pick one problem area (two at most) and keep the work focused on it.
  • You use communication analysis (replaying a recent argument line by line) and role-play to practise new ways of talking.

Common mistakes.

  • Drifting into unstructured supportive talk.
  • Trying to work on every relationship at once.
  • Forgetting the time limit, which is part of what makes IPT work.

Practice in India. IPT fits Indian life remarkably well because so much distress here is relational: marriage, in-laws, joint families, migration for work, a son leaving home, retirement from a respected role. Role disputes with in-laws and role transitions after marriage or childbirth are especially common in women. The WHO's Group IPT has been delivered successfully by trained non-specialists in low-resource settings, which makes it useful for community programmes.

Example. Shalini, 29, became depressed four months after giving birth. She lives with her husband's family. Her mother-in-law has taken over the baby's care and criticises her feeding. Her husband avoids the topic. IPT identifies two linked areas: a role transition (new mother, loss of her job and independence) and a role dispute (with her mother-in-law, and indirectly her husband). Sessions focus on what she has lost and gained, role-plays of a calm conversation with her mother-in-law, and one joint session with her husband to agree on how he will support her. After 14 sessions her EPDS score falls from 18 to 8.

7. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)

What it is. ACT, developed by Steven Hayes and colleagues, aims to build psychological flexibility: the ability to stay in contact with the present moment, including painful thoughts and feelings, and still act on what matters. Where CBT often asks "Is this thought true?", ACT asks "Is holding on to this thought helping you live the life you want?"

What it targets. Struggling against what cannot be changed. Many clients spend enormous energy trying to get rid of pain, anxiety or grief, and their lives shrink in the process.

The six core processes:

Process In plain words
AcceptanceMaking room for difficult feelings instead of fighting them
Cognitive defusionSeeing thoughts as thoughts, not commands or facts ("I'm having the thought that I'm useless")
Present-moment awarenessPaying attention to what is happening now
Self-as-contextNoticing that you are more than your thoughts and feelings
ValuesKnowing what really matters to you
Committed actionTaking steps toward those values, even with discomfort

Where it works. Chronic pain, chronic illness, burnout, caregiver stress, anxiety, depression, psychosis (as an add-on), and spiritual or existential distress. It is particularly useful when the problem cannot be removed: a lifelong illness, a disability, a loss that will not be undone.

What mastery looks like.

  • You teach through experience, not explanation: metaphors, exercises and in-session practice.
  • You do values work carefully, helping clients name their own values, not ones they think they should have.
  • You link every session to committed action in daily life. Acceptance without action is not ACT.
  • You model flexibility yourself, staying calm and curious with the client's distress.

Common mistakes.

  • Presenting acceptance as giving up or putting up with injustice. Acceptance is about inner experience, not about tolerating abuse or unfair situations.
  • Using metaphors as lectures.
  • Neglecting behaviour change.

Practice in India. Many ACT ideas will feel familiar to Indian clients. Acting on one's duty without clinging to the result echoes the Bhagavad Gita's idea of action without attachment to its fruits, and mindful awareness has deep roots in Indian traditions. Drawing these connections, where the client finds them meaningful, can make ACT feel less foreign. Be careful not to impose religious framing on clients who don't want it.

Example. Mr. Iyer, 58, has had chronic back pain for six years after spinal surgery. He has seen many doctors, stopped walking, stopped temple visits and given up his evening tuition classes, which he loved. Every conversation is about his pain. ACT helps him notice the thought "I can't do anything until the pain goes" and hold it more lightly. He names his values: teaching, his grandchildren, his faith. He restarts one tuition class a week, sitting down, and walks to the temple twice a week with a stick. His pain rating barely changes. His life changes a great deal.

8. Behavioural parent training and couple therapy

The last of the eight is really a pair. Many adult problems live inside relationships, and two relationships come up again and again in practice: parent and child, and partner and partner. You need one strong method for each.

Behavioural parent training

What it is. A group of programmes built on social learning principles that teach parents to change their child's behaviour by changing their own responses. The best known are Parent-Child Interaction Therapy (PCIT), Triple P and Incredible Years. They share the same core ingredients.

What it targets. The coercive cycle, first described by Gerald Patterson. A child misbehaves, the parent shouts or gives in, the child learns that escalating works, and both sides escalate further. Over time, most of the attention the child receives is negative.

Core ingredients:

  1. Positive attention first: daily "special time" of 10 to 15 minutes in which the child leads play and the parent describes, praises and does not direct.
  2. Specific praise: "Thank you for putting your shoes away", not "Good boy".
  3. Clear, calm instructions: one at a time, phrased as what to do, not what to stop.
  4. Planned ignoring of minor attention-seeking behaviour.
  5. Consistent, mild consequences for serious misbehaviour, such as a brief time-out or loss of a privilege, without shouting or hitting.
  6. Predictable routines for mornings, homework and bedtime.

Where it works. Child behaviour problems, ADHD-related behaviour, aggression, and parenting stress. It also helps adult clients whose own depression or anger is tangled up with a difficult child.

Common mistakes. Jumping to consequences before building positive attention, and inconsistency between the two parents or between parents and grandparents.

Practice in India. Grandparents and other relatives are often closely involved in child care, so involve whoever is actually parenting. Physical punishment is still common. Do not lecture, but explain plainly that it predicts worse behaviour over time and show what works instead.

Couple therapy: Integrative Behavioural Couple Therapy or Emotionally Focused Therapy

Choose one of these two and learn it well. Both have strong evidence.

  • Integrative Behavioural Couple Therapy (IBCT), from Andrew Christensen and Neil Jacobson, combines acceptance and change. It helps partners see their recurring conflict as a shared pattern rooted in real differences ("unified detachment"), builds empathy for each other's vulnerable feelings ("empathic joining"), and then teaches communication and problem-solving skills.
  • Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT), from Sue Johnson, sees couple distress as a negative cycle driven by attachment fears, typically one partner pursuing and the other withdrawing. The therapist helps each partner reach the softer emotions under the anger or distance and turn towards each other.

What mastery looks like in either. You stay balanced and never side with one partner. You keep attention on the pattern, not on who is right. You slow down heated exchanges and draw out the vulnerable feelings underneath.

A firm rule. Always screen each partner separately for violence before any joint session. Couple therapy is not appropriate when one partner is afraid of the other. That situation needs safety planning, not joint sessions.

Practice in India. Couples often arrive late, after years of distress, and frequently one partner (often the husband) is reluctant. Extended family may be part of the conflict. It is often useful to see the willing partner alone first, use MI to engage the reluctant one, and include family influence in the formulation.

Example. Ravi and Neha, married eight years, argue almost nightly about money and his parents. She criticises, he goes silent and leaves the room, she criticises more. Using EFT, the therapist helps Neha say what lies under her anger ("I feel alone, like I don't matter to you") and helps Ravi say what lies under his silence ("I shut down because I feel I can never get it right"). Once each sees the other's fear, the fights lose their heat. Later sessions use IBCT-style problem-solving to agree on a monthly budget and how much to share with his parents.

Combining the eight

Real clients rarely bring one clean problem. A depressed man is also drinking. A woman who self-harms also has a trauma history. This is where the eight become far more powerful together than apart, provided you combine them with a plan instead of mixing techniques at random.

Start with one question: what is mainly in the way?

If the main obstacle is... Start with...
Any risk to self or othersA safety plan, before anything else
The client is not sure they want to changeMotivational Interviewing
Emotions so intense that crises keep derailing sessionsDBT skills
Avoiding feared situations, rituals or reassurance-seekingExposure
Trauma memories and stuck beliefs such as self-blameCognitive Processing Therapy
Unhelpful thinking and inactivity: low mood, worry, low self-worthCBT with behavioural activation
A relationship conflict or life change that set things offInterpersonal Psychotherapy
Pain, illness or loss that cannot be changedACT
A child's behaviour or the couple relationship itselfParent training or couple therapy

Five rules for combining

  1. Let the formulation decide. Every technique must follow from your map of what keeps the problem going.
  2. Sequence, don't scramble. A reliable order is: safety, then engagement (MI), then stabilisation (DBT skills), then the main therapy, then relapse prevention.
  3. One main model at a time. Use a second method as a short module inside the first, not as a parallel therapy. Five techniques in one session confuses the client and dilutes each one.
  4. Tell the client what you're doing and why. "For the next few weeks we'll focus on handling the urges. Once those are steadier, we'll work on the memories."
  5. Measure throughout. If scores stall for four to six sessions, revisit the formulation before adding another technique.

Five cases

Depression with heavy drinking. Arjun, 34, has moderate depression and drinks most nights to sleep. Starting CBT straight away fails because he misses homework and two sessions after weekend binges. The therapist switches to three sessions of MI. Arjun sets his own goal of no weekday drinking. Then twelve sessions of CBT with behavioural activation follow, with a short CBT-I module for sleep. Mood and drinking improve together, because each was keeping the other going.

Self-harm with childhood abuse. Pooja, 24, self-harms during flashbacks of abuse by an uncle. Trauma processing too early would overwhelm her. Four months of DBT skills come first, until self-harm stops and she can tolerate distress. Then CPT works on her belief that "I must have encouraged it." This sequence mirrors established treatment models that add trauma-focused work to DBT once the client is stable.

Chronic pain with low mood. Rafiq, 51, a driver, has had knee pain for three years and has stopped working. ACT helps him stop waiting for the pain to disappear before living. Behavioural activation from CBT schedules a graded return to activity, and a joint session with his wife reduces her well-meant over-protection.

A child's aggression and a father's anger. Eight-year-old Kabir hits his sister and refuses homework. His father shouts and sometimes slaps him, then feels guilty. Parent training with both parents and the grandmother builds special time, praise and calm consequences. Because the father's temper keeps breaking the plan, he has six individual sessions of CBT for anger. The household calms, and so does Kabir.

Grief locked in by avoidance. Mrs. Deshpande, 62, lost her son in a road accident two years ago. She will not enter his room or travel on that road and cries daily. IPT for grief helps her mourn and rebuild her relationships with her daughter and her temple group. Gentle exposure to her son's room and photographs, planned together, ends the avoidance that has kept her grief raw.

The foundation under all eight

None of the eight works well on its own. Each sits on six habits that should be present with every client.

  1. A strong working alliance. Agree on goals and tasks, and check regularly how the client feels about the work. A short alliance check at the end of a session ("What was helpful today? What didn't fit?") catches problems early.
  2. Case formulation. A shared, written map of the problem: what made the person vulnerable, what triggered it, what keeps it going, and what protects them. Without it, technique choice is guesswork.
  3. Risk assessment and safety planning. Ask about suicidal thoughts at intake and whenever mood drops. Asking does not put the idea in someone's head. A written safety plan, made with the client, is one of the most effective brief interventions we have.
  4. Measurement-based care. Use short, validated scales (PHQ-9, GAD-7, or a problem-specific measure) every session or every few sessions, and look at the trend together. Clinicians are poor at spotting clients who are quietly getting worse. Numbers are not.
  5. Cultural adaptation. Ask how the client and their family understand the problem, what language they think in, who makes decisions, and what role faith plays. Adapt examples, metaphors and family involvement accordingly. Adapting the delivery is not the same as diluting the active ingredient.
  6. Supervision. Every method in this article is learned in supervised practice, not in a workshop. Regular supervision, ideally with recorded sessions, is the single best investment in your effectiveness.

A three-year learning path

Trying to learn all eight at once leads to the problem this article began with. Learn them in layers, each building on the last.

Year Learn Why this order
Year 1CBT (with behavioural activation, problem-solving and exposure), Motivational Interviewing, safety planning and measurement-based careThis alone lets you treat most depression and anxiety and engage ambivalent clients safely
Year 2DBT skills and Cognitive Processing TherapyThese cover the high-risk and trauma caseload that Year 1 cannot fully handle
Year 3IPT, ACT, and parent training or one couple therapyThese add depth for relational problems, chronic conditions and families
Year 4 onwardsOne specialisation, chosen from your caseloadOptions include Schema Therapy (personality), CBT for psychosis, EMDR, CBT-E (eating disorders) or child and adolescent work

How to learn each method properly

  1. Read the manual cover to cover. Not a summary, the actual treatment manual.
  2. Attend skills-based training with role-play, not lecture-only workshops.
  3. Treat supervised cases. A reasonable benchmark is 8 to 10 cases per method under supervision before working independently.
  4. Record and rate your sessions with a fidelity scale where one exists, such as the Cognitive Therapy Scale (Revised) for CBT or the MITI for Motivational Interviewing.
  5. Track your outcomes. Over time, your own data on client improvement will show you which methods you deliver well and which need more work.

If you also see children and adolescents

Add three methods to the core:

  • Trauma-Focused CBT (TF-CBT) for children after trauma, with a parallel parent component.
  • Parent-Child Interaction Therapy (PCIT) or another behavioural parent training programme, at a deeper level than the adult core requires.
  • CBT for child anxiety, such as Coping Cat, or SPACE (Supportive Parenting for Anxious Childhood Emotions) when the child will not engage but the parents will.

Closing: depth over breadth

Go back to Meera, the counsellor with fifteen certificates, and Rohan, who still could not ride the metro. Meera was not short of knowledge or care. She was short of depth in the one method Rohan needed.

The clients who come to us do not need a therapist who has heard of everything. They need one who can do a few things very well, combine them thoughtfully, measure whether they are working, and refer without hesitation when something else is needed. Eight methods, practised for years under supervision, will make you that kind of therapist.

The field will keep producing new therapies, and some of them will be valuable. Look at each one through the same lens: does it do something the core eight cannot? Most of the time, the answer will be no, and you can go back to getting better at what already works.

Learn a few therapies deeply, combine them wisely, and refer without ego.
