## PART 10 — THE COUNSELLING PROCESS MODEL (CSK)

### CSK-00 YourCounselor process model: Engage → Explore → Change
Most skills-based counselling models describe the same broad arc — build a relationship, understand the problem together, then work on change (compare Egan's Skilled Helper, Hill's Exploration–Insight–Action model, and similar staged models). Ours is organised for software as three stages, each with three steps.

| Stage | Purpose | Step 1 | Step 2 | Step 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1 Engage** | Form a working alliance | First contact and set-up | Opening session | Helping the client open up |
| **2 Explore** | Understand the problem and agree a formulation | Broad survey of the client's life and concerns | Narrowing down: testing ideas about what keeps the problem going | Agreed formulation and goals |
| **3 Change** | Create change and make it last | Active intervention | Ending | Independent self-management |

**Working rules:**
- Stages blend into one another; going back to an earlier stage is normal, not failure.
- Look for strengths at every stage.
- Set the pace by how much the client is suffering and how ready they are to reflect.

**App mapping:** each step is a workflow item with its own checklist and a "complete" status (see Appendix D, ProcessStage).

### CSK-01 STR record: the basic unit of case understanding
**S**ituation → **T**houghts (words and mental pictures) → **R**eactions (emotions, body sensations, what the person said and did).
- This is the familiar cognitive-behavioural sequence (Ellis's ABC; Beck's cognitive model), extended so every kind of thinking target in CSK-02 can be logged.
- **STR worksheet fields:** Situation (one line) · Thoughts (each tagged with a thinking-target type and a 0–100% belief rating) · Emotions (0–100) · Body sensations · Words and actions.

### CSK-02 Thinking targets (assessment and intervention taxonomy)
Six kinds of thinking that commonly keep problems going, each drawn from an established line of research.

| Target | Unhelpful form | Helpful form | Main methods | Roots in the literature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Rigid rules** | Absolute demands ("must", "should", "have to") | Flexible preferences ("I'd really like…, but I can cope if not") | Disputing (CSK-32); writing a flexible alternative | Ellis (REBT) |
| **Interpretations** | Mind-reading, leaping from facts to conclusions | Readings checked against evidence | Separating observation from inference; alternatives; behavioural experiments | Beck (cognitive therapy) |
| **Inner voice (self-talk)** | Harsh, catastrophic commentary | Coping self-statements matched to each phase of a stressful event | Stress-inoculation scripts (CSK-32) | Meichenbaum |
| **Mental pictures** | Vivid images of failure or danger | Coping and mastery images | Imagery rehearsal; image rescripting | Imagery work in CBT |
| **Attributions** | Blaming only oneself, only others, or unchangeable traits | Balanced, changeable explanations | Evidence review; responsibility pie; focus on controllables | Weiner; attributional retraining |
| **Predictions** | Overestimating danger, underestimating one's coping | Realistic odds and confidence in coping | Probability estimates; graded tests | Bandura (self-efficacy) |

Related everyday skills: setting realistic goals, making decisions, and anticipating problems (CSK-32).

**Observable behaviour channels (for assessment and feedback):** what is said (words) · how it is said (volume, pace, tone, emphasis, clarity) · body language (face, gaze, posture, gesture, distance) · physical contact · actions taken.

## PART 11 — ENGAGE-STAGE SKILLS (CSK-10 to CSK-19)

### CSK-10 Before the first session
- **Setting:** private, comfortable, safe and accessible, with clear directions and secure record-keeping.
- **Publicity:** factual only — no promised results and no client testimonials (see ETH).
- **First contact (call, message or form):** collect only essential details, give practical information (time, fees, session length, limits of confidentiality) and check for crisis.
- **Records:** intake data, notes and correspondence stored securely; clients can access them as the law allows.

### CSK-11 Core attending and understanding competencies (checklist)
| # | Competency | What it looks like | Typical slip |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Non-judgemental acceptance | Treats the problem as the client's to own and explore | Lecturing, moralising, telling the client what they should do |
| 2 | Staying in the client's frame | Responses follow the client's viewpoint and priorities | Jumping to advice, interpretation, reassurance or labels |
| 3 | Hearing the voice | Notices loudness, speed, pitch, stress and hesitations | Attending only to the words |
| 4 | Reading the body | Notices facial expression, gaze, posture, gestures, distance, grooming | Missing a mismatch between words and body |
| 5 | Encouraging and opening | Brief prompts ("mm-hm", "and then?", repeating a key word) and open questions ("What was that like?") | Closed or loaded questions ("That must have been great, right?") |
| 6 | Paraphrasing | Restates the gist in new words, often starting "You…" | Repeating word for word, or adding one's own meaning |
| 7 | Reflecting feelings | Names the feeling at the right strength, with its cause: "You're feeling X because Y" | Wrong intensity; starting every reflection the same way |
| 8 | Managing one's own reactions | Uses private reminders ("Listen first"; "This is their problem to solve") | Letting one's own anxiety steer the conversation |
| 9 | Working with reluctance | See CSK-12 | Blaming the client |
| 10 | Sensitivity to context and difference | Considers culture, caste, gender, sexuality, class, age, disability, religion | Assuming the client is like oneself |

**Reflecting feelings well — four things to get right:**
- Strength: pick the word that matches intensity (e.g., "irritated" vs "furious").
- Mixture: clients often feel more than one thing at once.
- Cause: link the feeling to what triggered it.
- Unspoken feelings: name implied emotions only once trust allows (advanced empathy).

**Feeling-word bank (for app pickers):**
- **Pleasant:** appreciated, calm, cared for, cheerful, confident, content, encouraged, grateful, hopeful, proud, relieved, safe
- **Unpleasant:** afraid, alone, angry, anxious, ashamed, betrayed, bitter, confused, defeated, discouraged, embarrassed, envious, guilty, helpless, hurt, humiliated, inadequate, irritated, low, neglected, overwhelmed, rejected, sad, tense, trapped, uneasy, worried

### CSK-12 Reluctance and resistance
- **Common reasons:**
  - Mixed feelings about change; fear of what may come out
  - Being sent rather than choosing to come (by parents, school, employer or court)
  - A counsellor whose style is very different from what the client expected
  - Feeling unheard
  - Counsellor tiredness, irritation or bias
- **Helpful responses:**
  - Reflect the client's frustration without defending yourself.
  - Side with the client's experience (e.g., "Being made to come here when you feel your teachers are unfair is really annoying").
  - Make it clear the client can say no.
  - Invite, don't insist.
  - Link the work to what the client wants for themselves.
  - Acknowledge even small disclosures.
  - Earn trust before challenging.
- **Principle:** reluctance is an expected part of counselling. Check your own approach before labelling the client "resistant".

### CSK-13 Opening the first session
**Meeting the client:**
1. Take a moment to settle yourself after the previous session.
2. Collect the client from the waiting area yourself.
3. Greet them by name with a warm expression.
4. Introduce yourself.
5. Walk in together and show them where to sit.

**Opening words:**
- Set the time frame ("We have about 45 minutes today…").
- Invite the client to begin, for example: "Where would you like to start?" · "Your doctor suggested you see me — how do you see things?" · "You look quite troubled. Would you like to tell me what's been happening?"

**Setting out the plan:** briefly describe the process (we'll understand what's going on, agree what to work on, then practise ways to change it) and check the client is comfortable with that.
- If several problems come up: summarise them → ask which matters most right now → pick one concrete recent example.

**Summaries:** use short summaries to mark transitions within a session.

**Agreement:** agree on process, roles, fees, likely number of sessions and confidentiality limits. The format may be formal or informal, but *informed consent is always required* (ETH-01).

### CSK-14 Referral
- **Refer when:** the difficulty is outside your competence (e.g., psychosis, complex trauma, eating disorders); a colleague's skills fit better; medical or psychiatric assessment is needed; there is a conflict of interest.
- **Options:** hand the client over completely, or continue working alongside another professional.
- **Steps:** explain why; offer choices; get consent to share information; make the handover; check it happened.
- Principle: know where your competence ends and who does what well (a point stressed by Lazarus in multimodal therapy).

### CSK-15 Crisis counselling protocol (works with ASM-06)
**A crisis** is a state in which the demands on a person feel beyond their ability to cope. Signs include:
- Physical: exhaustion, sleeplessness, agitation
- Emotional: shock, panic, despair, rage
- Cognitive: tunnel vision, feeling powerless, hopelessness
- Behavioural: withdrawal or frantic activity; increased risk of harm to self or others

**Steps:**
1. **Prepare in advance:** know your back-up (doctors, emergency psychiatric services, crisis lines) and the limits of your role.
2. **Stay calm:** warm, steady and clear.
3. **Listen:** let the client release feeling and respond with empathy.
4. **Assess risk:** check contact with reality; *ask directly about suicide* (asking does not plant the idea; avoiding the question increases danger); check risk to others.
5. **Find supports and strengths:** "Who could be with you or help you today?"
6. **Clarify the problem:** lower emotional intensity first, then gently question hopeless conclusions.
7. **Make a concrete plan:** for example, "Your friend will drive you home and stay with you tonight, and I'll call you at 9 a.m. Is there anything that could get in the way of that?" Mobilise resources.
8. **Be clear about contact:** next appointment and how to reach help in an emergency.
9. **Record everything and consult a supervisor.**

## PART 12 — EXPLORE-STAGE SKILLS (CSK-20 to CSK-29)

### CSK-20 Questioning
- **Three layers of information:**
  - What the client offers freely
  - What careful questions bring out
  - What is hidden or outside awareness
- **Decisions to make:** why you are asking (never just to fill silence); present vs past focus; breadth vs depth; order of topics.
- **Question types:**
  - Open and closed
  - Clarifying ("When you say 'fed up', what does that mean for you?")
  - Expanding ("Say a bit more about that.")
  - Specifying (who, what, when, where, how often)
  - Emotion-focused ("What did you feel then?")
  - Body-focused ("What did you notice in your body?")
  - Thought-focused ("What was going through your mind?")
  - Action-focused ("What did you say or do next?")
  - Solution-focused (INT-17)
  - Rating (0–10)
- **Style:**
  - One question at a time.
  - Alternate questions with reflections so it doesn't feel like an interrogation.
  - Prefer "what led to…" over "why…", which tends to make people defensive.

### CSK-21 Challenging
- **Encourage ownership language:** "I forgot to pay the bill" rather than "The bill didn't get paid."
- **Point out mismatches** between:
  - Words and tone or body ("You say it doesn't bother you, but your voice sounds shaky")
  - Different parts of what the client says
  - What the client says and what they do
  - What they agreed and what they followed through on
  - What they said before and what they say now
  - Their account and the available evidence
  - Their view and other people's views
- **Question distorted interpretations:** offer another reading, or ask what evidence supports theirs.
- **Guidelines:**
  - Challenge from a base of understanding and in a tentative tone ("I'm noticing two things that seem to pull in different directions…").
  - Use sparingly.
  - Let the client respond.
  - The aim is to help, never to win.

### CSK-22 Giving clients feedback
- Speak for yourself ("I noticed…").
- Be concrete and constructive ("try speaking a little slower and looking up more", not "that was poor").
- Mention what went well as well as what to change.
- Share your own reaction when it helps ("I felt a bit shut out when…").
- Show what you mean.
- Ask how the client sees it.
- After a role-play, ask the client to assess themselves *before* you comment.

### CSK-23 Counsellor self-disclosure
- **Showing you are affected:**
  - To news the client shares: "That's wonderful" / "I'm really sorry that happened"
  - To the client as a person: "It took real courage to do that"
  - To their distress: "I'm concerned about how hard this has been for you"
- **Sharing your own experience:** short, relevant, quickly handed back to the client, and never a burden to them.
- **Avoid:** frequent or long disclosures, disclosing issues you haven't resolved, disclosing to meet your own needs.
- With teenagers, straightforward, non-evasive answers to personal questions build trust (CHD-03).

### CSK-24 Assessing emotions and body reactions
- **Why it matters:** emotions power change; feelings that go unacknowledged block it.
- **Body reactions to ask about:** heartbeat, breathing, muscle tension, sleep, appetite, energy, sexual interest, stomach and bowel symptoms.
- **Aspects of emotion to assess:**
  - How often, how strong, how long
  - Ability to feel (numbed vs overwhelmed)
  - How feelings are expressed (appropriately or not)
  - Anxiety and self-protective defences
  - Mixed feelings
  - Feelings linked to risk (suicidal or homicidal)
- **Methods:**
  - Open, emotion-focused questions
  - Advanced empathy (naming implied feelings)
  - Focusing attention ("Stay with that feeling for a moment — where do you notice it?")
  - Role-play to bring feelings into the room
  - Emotion log: situation · feeling · strength 0–10 · body reaction
- **Medication:** record current prescriptions, recognise when medical review is needed, and liaise with the prescriber.

### CSK-25 Assessing thinking
- Draw out thoughts and images with STR questions ("What went through your mind?", "What picture came up?", "What did you say to yourself?").
- Tag each thought with a thinking-target type (CSK-02).
- Rate how strongly it is believed (0–100%).
- Useful methods: thinking aloud, replaying the situation through role-play, and thought logs between sessions.
- **Build hypotheses:** which rigid rules, interpretations, inner voice, mental pictures, attributions or predictions keep the problem going? Offer them tentatively and check with the client.

### CSK-26 Assessing words and actions
- **Channels:** words, voice, body, physical contact, actions (CSK-02).
- **Methods:** client report, observing role-plays, self-monitoring logs, reports from others.
- **Key question:** is this a skill the client lacks, or one they have but don't use?

### CSK-27 Agreed formulation (end of the Explore stage)
**Steps:**
1. Keep notes throughout.
2. Summarise the problem situation.
3. Offer hypotheses about the thinking targets and behaviour patterns involved.
4. Ask for the client's view and revise together.
5. Turn each pattern into a **goal**.
6. Record the result in the form below.

**Form (app record):**

| Problem / situation | Thinking targets: current pattern → goal | Words & actions: current pattern → goal |
|---|---|---|

**Closing the first session:**
- Summarise.
- Agree next steps and any starter homework (e.g., self-monitoring).
- Ask how the session felt.
- Book the next appointment.

## PART 13 — CHANGE-STAGE SKILLS (CSK-30 to CSK-45)

### CSK-30 Planning interventions
- **Choose methods according to:** the client's goals and preferences, research evidence, your competence, the client's readiness and learning style, and cultural fit.
- **Plan styles:** structured (sessions mapped out in advance) or flexible (skills taken up as they arise).
- **Points to consider:**
  - Work on thinking first when it is blocking action; work on both together when they feed each other.
  - Go from easier to harder.
  - Number and length of sessions.
  - Who else should be involved (family, school, workplace).

### CSK-31 Teaching skills: explain → demonstrate → practise
1. **Explain:** a short, clear, jargon-free explanation with examples; check the client has understood.
2. **Demonstrate:** model the skill live, on video, in writing or through a script; for thinking skills, model your self-talk out loud.
3. **Practise:** rehearse, give feedback, repeat, and build up gradually.
4. **Agree homework** (CSK-40).

**Structure of a typical middle session:**
1. Check in ("What's happened since we last met?")
2. Review homework
3. Agree today's agenda together
4. Work on one skill: explain → demonstrate → practise
5. Summarise
6. Agree homework
7. Close

### CSK-32 Working with thinking
| Target | Procedure |
|---|---|
| **Rigid rules** | Spot the demand → question it from four angles: *usefulness* ("Is holding this rule helping you?"), *evidence* ("What shows it has to be this way?"), *logic* ("Does wanting something make it a must?"), *perspective* ("Could life still be worthwhile if this didn't happen?") → write a flexible-preference version → rehearse it → homework (after Ellis's disputing methods) |
| **Interpretations** | Separate what happened from what was assumed → list other possible readings → choose the one the evidence best supports → test it with a behavioural experiment (predict, try, compare) |
| **Inner voice** | Build a **coping-statement card** for four moments of a stressful event (after Meichenbaum's stress inoculation training): *Preparing* ("I've planned what to say") · *Facing it* ("One step at a time; breathe slowly") · *If it gets hard* ("Feeling nervous is expected; I can pause and carry on") · *Afterwards* ("I handled that better than I feared") → combine with relaxation → rehearse aloud or with a recording |
| **Mental pictures** | Rehearse coping and success in imagery; calming imagery; replacing a distressing image with a more helpful one |
| **Attributions** | Look at the evidence for causes; divide responsibility fairly (e.g., a responsibility pie chart); focus on what can be influenced |
| **Predictions** | Estimate realistic odds; list resources and supports; weigh likely gains and losses; test in small steps |
| **Realistic goals** | SMART: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound — and owned by the client |
| **Decisions** | Phase 1: face the decision (options, information, pros and cons, choose). Phase 2: put it into action and review |
| **Relapse prevention** | Identify high-risk situations → plan how to cope → treat a slip as information, not a collapse (after Marlatt) |

### CSK-33 Working with words and actions
- **Self-monitoring:** counts, logs and charts (e.g., daily log of assertive requests made).
- **Building awareness:** feedback on voice and body; video feedback.
- **Rehearsal and role-play:** set the scene → counsellor demonstrates → client practises → feedback → swap roles → repeat → plan real-life use.
- **Activity scheduling:** an hour-by-hour weekly plan balancing enjoyment and achievement — the heart of behavioural activation for depression.
- **Graded tasks:** break a goal into small steps from easiest to hardest.
- **Experiments:** "If I try X in situation Y, I expect Z." Do it, then review.
- **Structured activities and games:** particularly for groups and younger clients.
- **Self-reward:** the client picks rewards that follow only after the target behaviour, plus genuine self-praise.
- **Support:** identify helpers (family, friends, peer groups, self-help resources).

### CSK-34 Working with emotions
- **Allowing feelings:**
  - Create a safe climate and give permission.
  - Direct attention to bodily sensations.
  - Use empty-chair or role-play (INT-05).
  - Help the client notice feelings they usually block.
- **Expressing feelings:**
  - Assertive statements: "I feel… when… because…"
  - Voice and body that fit the message.
  - Rehearsal.
- **Regulating feelings:**
  - Relaxation (CSK-35; Core 1 INT-07).
  - Work on the thoughts behind the feeling.
  - Anger management: know your triggers → rethink the situation → calming self-talk → take a break → express assertively.
  - Stress management and problem solving.
  - Suicidal feelings: follow ASM-06.
- **Medication:** refer for medical review in severe depression, psychosis, severe anxiety or possible bipolar disorder; psychological work continues alongside.

### CSK-35 Systematic desensitisation — full protocol (expands INT-03a)
**1. Progressive muscle relaxation (after Jacobson; Bernstein & Borkovec):**
- Tense each muscle group for about 5–7 seconds, then let go for 20–30 seconds, noticing the difference.
- **Order:** dominant hand and forearm → dominant upper arm → other hand and forearm → other upper arm → forehead → eyes and nose → cheeks and jaw → neck → chest, shoulders and upper back → stomach → dominant thigh → calf → foot → other thigh → calf → foot.
- Practise twice a day, about 15 minutes each time.
- Shorten step by step: grouped muscles → relaxing by recall → counting down → a single cue word such as "relax".

**2. Building the hierarchy:**
- 10–15 situations, each rated 0–100 SUDS, with even gaps (about 10–15 points apart).
- Organise by distance (e.g., closer and closer to a dog) or by time (e.g., a week before an exam, the night before, the morning of).

**3. Working through it:**
- Client relaxes → imagines the lowest item for 5–10 seconds → raises a finger if anxious → the scene is stopped and the client relaxes again.
- Repeat until the item produces no anxiety twice in a row, then move up.
- Cover no more than 3–4 items per session and begin each session one step below where the last finished.

**4. Real-life practice:** graded real exposure following the same hierarchy, usually after imaginal work.

**Evidence:** A (exposure-based treatment of phobias).

### CSK-40 Agreeing homework (all modalities)
1. **Explain the purpose:** why practice between sessions matters.
2. **Agree a realistic task:** builds on what was learned; at the right difficulty; a small, definite commitment beats a big, vague one.
3. **Make instructions clear:** what, when, how often and how it will be recorded. Have the client write it down, check it, and provide any forms.
4. **Plan rewards and anticipate obstacles.**
5. **Review it next session, without exception** — homework that is never reviewed soon stops being done.

For children, call it "practice" or a "challenge", and involve parents (CHD).

### CSK-41 Session length, frequency and number
- **Length:**
  - Usual: 45–50 minutes.
  - First session: up to 90–120 minutes if needed.
  - Brief review sessions: 20–30 minutes.
  - Keep to the length you agreed.
- **Frequency:** normally weekly at a regular time; more often in crisis or before a deadline; spaced out towards the end.
- **Number:**
  - Short-term: 1–10
  - Medium-term: 11–20
  - Long-term: more than 20 (e.g., long-standing deprivation or personality difficulties)
  - Influenced by: client goals, progress and finances; counsellor capacity; agency or insurer limits.
- **Contact between sessions:** only for a clear purpose (checking safety, adjusting homework), with agreed limits.

### CSK-42 Tracking progress
- **Sources of information:**
  - What the client reports
  - Homework records
  - Reports from others
  - Performance in role-plays
  - Countable targets (e.g., cigarettes per day)
  - Repeated standard measures (PHQ-9, GAD-7, DASS-21)
  - Brief session-by-session client feedback on outcome and alliance (e.g., ORS/SRS-type ratings — check licence terms before digitising named measures)
- **Track two things separately:** (a) movement towards goals, and (b) how well the client understands and uses the skills.
- **When progress stalls:** after an agreed period without change, revisit the formulation, the alliance, the client's readiness and the fit of the method; consult a supervisor; consider referral.

### CSK-43 Ending well and self-management
**When to end:**
- Goals have been met and both of you believe the client can manage.
- The client is using the skills independently.
- Or external limits apply.
- Raise the topic of ending *well before* the last session.

**Ending formats:**

| Format | When it fits |
|---|---|
| Fixed number (e.g., 8 sessions) | Clear goals; limits dependency |
| Open-ended (until goals are met) | Complex or evolving issues |
| **Tapering** (weekly → fortnightly → monthly) | Best for consolidating skills |
| Booster session(s), e.g., after 3 months | Check maintenance; no new material |
| Follow-up call or message | Feedback and a reminder |

**Tasks at the end:**
- Look back at progress.
- Strengthen skills: over-practise; use them in different situations; anticipate setbacks; plan how to "be your own counsellor".
- Talk about feelings about ending.
- Arrange any further help needed.
- Ask for the client's evaluation of the counselling.
- Close the records.

**After counselling, the client:**
- Keeps using the skills.
- Keeps an eye on progress.
- Gets back on track after slips.
- Weaves the skills into daily life.
- Draws on support.
- Knows they can come back if needed.

## PART 14 — PROFESSIONAL PRACTICE (CSK-50s)

### CSK-50 Core ethical principles (complements ETH-01)
Most professional codes (e.g., the BACP Ethical Framework, APA Ethics Code, RCI guidance) rest on a similar set of principles:
- **Trustworthiness (fidelity):** keep faith with clients; protect confidentiality.
- **Autonomy:** freely given, informed consent; declare conflicts of interest.
- **Beneficence:** practise within competence; use supervision and CPD; monitor outcomes.
- **Non-maleficence:** no sexual, financial or emotional exploitation; do not practise when impaired; act on colleagues' malpractice.
- **Justice:** fair access; no discrimination; obey the law.
- **Self-respect:** look after yourself — personal support, therapy, a life outside work.

**Frequent dilemma areas:** competence · limits of confidentiality · dual relationships · touch · gifts · fees · attraction to clients · records · online practice · training (e.g., practising skills on peers, personal disclosure in training groups).

### CSK-51 Ethical decision-making (app wizard)
A combined model drawing on published decision-making frameworks (e.g., Corey, Corey & Callanan; Bond):
1. Describe the situation and the question.
2. Ask whose dilemma it is and who is affected.
3. Identify the ethical principles, codes and laws involved.
4. Consult a supervisor or colleague.
5. List possible courses of action.
6. Weigh the likely consequences of each and choose.
7. Act, and record what you decided and why.
8. Review the outcome afterwards.

**Self-check:** notice when your own rigid rules ("My supervisor must never think badly of me"), self-serving interpretations, avoidance or haste are shaping the decision.

### CSK-52 Diversity-sensitive practice
- **Known critiques of mainstream counselling:** built on Western, individualistic, middle-class and male assumptions; single-culture viewpoint; blind to discrimination and oppression.
- **Multicultural competence (Sue and colleagues):**
  1. Awareness of your own cultural background and biases
  2. Knowledge of the client's worldview
  3. Skills and methods that fit the client's culture
- **Practice points:**
  - Ask about cultural identity rather than assuming it.
  - Recognise the pull between collective and individual values.
  - Adjust communication — directness, eye contact, silence, touch.
  - Involve family where that is culturally valued and the client agrees.
  - Acknowledge discrimination when it is part of the story.
  - Use a trained interpreter, **never a relative or a child**.
- **Gender-aware practice:**
  - Explore how gender expectations shape the problem (e.g., men discouraged from showing emotion; women carrying most caregiving).
  - Stay alert to violence and abuse.
  - Respect sexual and gender diversity.
- **India notes:**
  - Joint and extended family dynamics.
  - Stigma and fear of social judgement ("log kya kahenge").
  - Distress expressed through the body.
  - Spiritual explanations and traditional healers.
  - Diversity of caste, class and language.
  - Core 1 ASM-02 applies: no stereotyped assumptions.

### CSK-53 Supervision
- **Purpose:** help the counsellor develop an *internal supervisor* — the ability to reflect on and guide their own work.
- **What supervision covers:**
  - Client safety and quality of service
  - Thinking like a scientist: forming and testing hypotheses
  - Relationship, assessment and intervention skills
  - Using models flexibly
  - Ethics and diversity
  - Emotional support and availability in a crisis
  - Self-monitoring and honest self-evaluation
  - Using research
  - Recognising limits
  - Noticing when one's own needs distort how a client is seen
  - The counsellor's own thinking patterns
  - Getting the most from supervision time
- **Formats:** one-to-one, group, peer, live, audio/video review (with client consent), transcripts.
- **Presenting a case:**
  1. Anonymised background
  2. Referral question
  3. Formulation (STR record and thinking targets)
  4. Interventions so far
  5. A recording or transcript extract
  6. A specific question for the supervisor
- **Pitfalls:** misuse of power, anxiety about evaluation, supervisees hiding material, boundary problems. Use a written agreement covering goals, frequency, confidentiality, evaluation and emergency access.

### CSK-54 Personal therapy and CPD
- **Personal therapy or self-development:** builds self-knowledge, resolves personal issues and gives first-hand experience of being a client.
- **CPD:** workshops, courses, reading, research, supervision, peer consultation.
- **CPD log (app feature):** date, activity, hours, reflection, how it will change practice.

## PART 15 — CHILD & ADOLESCENT PRACTICE (CHD)

### CHD-00 Core principles
1. **Children are not miniature adults.** Their development, surroundings and the way they arrive in therapy all change what therapy involves.
2. **Separate ordinary developmental struggles from disorder.** Many difficulties — tantrums, particular fears, bed-wetting, sleep problems — fade as the child matures.
3. **Thoughtful integration:** combine theory-based methods and work across several systems (child, family, school).
4. **Evidence:** meta-analyses of child therapy report medium overall effects (d around .70), meaning the average treated child does better than most untreated children. Cognitive-behavioural methods have the strongest support (Weisz et al.).
5. **Use both evidence-based treatments and practice-based evidence** — i.e., proven protocols plus regular feedback measures for the individual child (e.g., child-friendly outcome ratings).

### CHD-01 Matching methods to developmental level
| Stage (Piaget) | Approx. age | What the child can do | Methods that fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sensorimotor / preoperational | 0–7 | Sees from own viewpoint; thinks concretely; limited use of words to guide behaviour | Play therapy, parent-led approaches, behavioural methods, very simple coping phrases |
| Concrete operational | 7–11 | Reasons logically about real, concrete events; struggles with hypotheticals | CBT using concrete materials, worksheets, stories, games and role-play; **simple disputing from about age 8** |
| Formal operational | 12+ | Abstract, hypothetical and self-reflective thinking | CBT, REBT and IPT close to adult form; insight work; Socratic dialogue |

**Rules:**
- Never set goals beyond the child's developmental level.
- Assess cognitive and language level early.
- Screen for developmental delay.
- Avoid the **"uniformity myth"** (Kiesler) — children of the same age with the same problem are not interchangeable.

**Other frameworks to draw on:** Erikson's psychosocial stages, Kohlberg's moral stages (Core 1 INS-13), identity and ethnic-identity development (CHD-21).

### CHD-02 How therapy with children differs
| Issue | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Motivation:** usually brought by adults and may say nothing is wrong | The first goal is just a good relationship and willingness to return; agree goals the child cares about alongside the adults' goals |
| **Ideas about therapy:** may fear being called "mad" or think the counsellor works for parents or school | Explain simply what counselling is and isn't; check their understanding again later |
| **Language:** limited vocabulary; may agree to words they don't understand | Use the child's own words for things; teach feeling words; use play, drawing and other non-verbal media; check understanding often |
| **Dependence on others:** cannot change their school, family or home | Involve parents and teachers; sometimes the goal is *coping with* something that cannot change (e.g., a parent's drinking) |
| **Personality still forming** | More room for change (good for prevention), but moods and behaviour swing more |
| **Teenagers:** impatient, wary, give short answers | See CHD-03 |

### CHD-03 Engaging adolescents
- Begin with everyday, non-threatening topics. Early on, avoid deep probing, demands to explain misbehaviour, and long silences.
- **Counsellor style:**
  - More active and conversational — closer to a trusted older mentor than a distant expert.
  - Genuine; answer personal questions in a straightforward way.
  - Share views without imposing values.
  - Know something about current youth culture.
- Indirect probes and interpretations can feel like tricks and increase wariness, so be open and concrete.
- **Show you care through action** — a real commitment to sorting things out together.
- **Balance independence and support:** don't treat teenagers like young children, and don't hand them total freedom.
- The first agreement may simply be to come back once more.
- Think in **stages of change** (Prochaska & DiClemente): most young people arrive not yet considering change. Build motivation first (motivational interviewing; the motivation check in INT-15) before teaching action techniques.

### CHD-04 Multimodal and multisystem assessment
**BASIC ID (Lazarus), extended for children as BASIC IDEAL (Keat):**

| Letter | Area |
|---|---|
| B | Behaviour |
| A | Affect (emotions) |
| S | Sensations |
| I | Imagery |
| C | Cognition |
| I | Interpersonal relationships |
| D | Drugs, diet and physical health |
| E | Education / school |
| A | Adults in the child's life |
| L | Learning the child's culture |

**Use:** build a grid of problems against these areas and plan an intervention for each cell (child, parent, teacher, school or medical).

**Multisystemic Therapy principles** (Henggeler and colleagues; for serious antisocial behaviour or complex cases):
- Map risk, protective and maintaining factors across child, family, peers, school and community.
- Goals set with the family.
- Caregivers are the main agents of change.
- Build on strengths.
- Focus on the present and on action.
- Target the sequences of interaction between systems.
- Deliver help where the family lives, learns and works.

### CHD-05 Extra intake items for children (add to ASM-01)
- **Informants:** the child, each parent or guardian, teacher (with consent).
- **Custody and guardianship:** who can consent; court orders; the non-custodial parent's rights of access.
- **Developmental history:** pregnancy and birth, milestones, temperament, illnesses and hospital stays.
- **School:** marks, attendance, behaviour, special-education support or IEP, friendships, bullying.
- **Family:** structure (nuclear or joint), changes (separation, moves, deaths), parenting and discipline style, mental health in the family.
- **The child's own view:** what they think the problem is and what they would like to be different (e.g., "If you had three wishes…").
- **Screens:** abuse and neglect, self-harm, substance use (adolescents), screen time and online safety, sleep.
- **Functional Behaviour Assessment (FBA)** for behaviour problems: define the behaviour precisely → what comes before → what follows → how often → likely purpose (gaining attention / escaping something / getting an item or activity / sensory) → a behaviour support plan with *replacement behaviours* that genuinely serve the child, not just the adults.

## PART 16 — CHILD & ADOLESCENT ETHICS AND LAW (CHD-10s)

### CHD-10 Who is the client?
Three parties are involved: the child, the parents or guardians, and the school or agency.
- The **child's welfare comes first** (you are their advocate).
- **Parents hold the legal rights** for a minor.
- Make lines of responsibility clear at the start. The younger the child, the greater your obligations to the parents.

**When a child refuses therapy, weigh:** age, cognitive maturity, how disturbed the child is, how disturbed the parents are, and how necessary treatment is.

### CHD-11 Rights
- **The child's rights:**
  - Information they can understand about assessment, findings, treatment and the reasons for it
  - To know where confidentiality ends
  - Some say over what is shared (ethically, if not always legally)
  - To decline or stop therapy where possible (not when a court or school plan requires it)
  - To be treated with respect and honesty
  - To take part in setting goals
  - Not to be made the family's scapegoat
- **Parents' rights:**
  - Legal responsibility for the child's welfare
  - Access to information
  - To seek and consent to treatment
  - To be involved in goals
  - To authorise release of information

### CHD-12 Confidentiality with minors — working rules
1. At the **first session, explain to the child and parents together** what stays private and what must be shared: danger to self, danger to others, abuse, and serious risk-taking.
2. **Standard agreement:** parents hear *general themes and progress*, not specifics; anything more is shared only with the child's agreement.
3. Before sharing reports with teachers or other professionals, get the parents' written consent and ask the child's agreement.
4. **Grey areas** (e.g., experimenting with alcohol or sex): decide by age, maturity, level of danger, local law and school policy; consult and record your reasoning.
5. **Groups:** explain confidentiality in child-friendly language ("You can tell people what we did, but not who said what"). Legal privilege may not protect things said in groups.
6. **Digital messages:** warn families about the privacy risks of email and messaging apps; use secure channels.
7. **Records:** keep private process notes separate from official progress notes. Parents usually have access to school or clinic records. Assume any note could be requested by a court.

### CHD-13 Duties to warn, protect and inform
- **Risk to others (the *Tarasoff* principle):**
  - Usually applies when there is a direct communication (including one reported by a family member), a serious and imminent threat, and an identifiable victim.
  - Actions: warn the person at risk (and their parents if they are a minor) → inform the authorities → take protective steps → consult → document.
- **Risk to self:**
  - *Always tell parents* when a minor is at risk of suicide, in a supportive way.
  - Ask parents to sign an acknowledgement.
  - Give home-safety advice (supervision; **removing means** — medicines, weapons, pesticides, cords and ropes).
  - Refer for specialist assessment.
  - If parents do not act, escalate to child-protection services.
  - Informing parents alone is not enough.
- **Threats of school violence:** assess history, triggers and protective factors; consult; inform the parents of both the threatened student and the student making the threat.
- **Abuse:** report on **reasonable suspicion** — you do not need proof. People who report in good faith are protected. Do not investigate yourself; contact parents only as the child-protection authority advises.
  - **India:** under the POCSO Act, 2012, reporting child sexual abuse is mandatory and failing to report is an offence. Report to the Special Juvenile Police Unit or local police, or call Child Helpline 1098 (see `crisis-resources.md`). The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 covers children in need of care and protection. *Verify current procedures.*

### CHD-14 Informed consent for minors
- **Three ingredients:** understanding, capacity, and freedom from pressure.
- **Valid consent covers:**
  - What the treatment involves, with its risks, discomforts and benefits
  - Alternatives
  - A chance to ask questions
  - The right to withdraw at any time
- **Written parental consent** is required for ongoing therapy with a minor; the young person's **assent** should be sought as an ethical matter.
- **Exceptions:** emergencies (e.g., assessing suicide risk or abuse) may go ahead first, with parents told immediately afterwards; emancipated minors; children in state care.
- **Teenagers who come on their own:** hold one meeting to check safety, then explain why parental consent is needed.
- **Tell families** if you are in training (and who supervises you), if a treatment is experimental, or if you are new to a particular area.
- **Medication:** school staff must not diagnose or press for medication; that decision belongs to parents and the doctor. Know the relevant policies.
- **India:** the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 treats people under 18 as minors who act through a guardian or nominated representative for treatment decisions. Good adolescent practice still seeks the young person's agreement. *Verify.*

### CHD-15 Competence and other obligations
- Training with adults does not qualify you to work with children; get child-specific training or refer.
- **Areas needing specialist skill or referral:** autism, ADHD, selective mutism, eating disorders, bipolar disorder, self-injury, substance use, trauma, and clients from linguistically or culturally different backgrounds.
- **Dual relationships:** weigh the power difference, how long the relationship lasts and how it will end (Gottlieb's model). Coaching a sports team that includes a client is not automatically wrong, but never discuss therapy outside sessions. **Sexual relationships are always forbidden.**
- **Private practice:** never recruit private clients from your institutional job (e.g., the school where you work).
- **Behaviour plans:** use the *least restrictive, least aversive, most effective* option. Aversive methods are ethically and legally questionable and no more effective than positive alternatives.
- **Monitor outcomes:** change or stop an intervention if the data show it isn't helping.

## PART 17 — CHILD & ADOLESCENT INTERVENTION LIBRARY (INT continued)

### INT-13 Child-Centred Play Therapy (CCPT)
- **Ages:** roughly 3–10 (adapted forms exist for pre-teens and adolescents).
- **Evidence:** A–B. Meta-analytic estimates: play therapy overall d ≈ .80, humanistic approaches higher (Bratton et al., 2005); CCPT specifically d ≈ .47 (Lin & Bratton, 2015). School-based studies show gains in behaviour problems, emotional difficulties, self-efficacy and academic outcomes, with support across cultures.
- **Level:** L2 with specific play-therapy training.
- **Theory:** Rogers' person-centred theory. Difficulties arise when a child's experience clashes with the self-picture they have absorbed from others' conditions of worth. Healing comes from a relationship that offers the "necessary and sufficient" conditions: psychological contact, a therapist who is genuine, unconditional positive regard, accurate empathy, and a child who experiences that acceptance and understanding.

**Axline's principles, summarised:** a warm, friendly relationship; accepting the child exactly as they are; a permissive atmosphere; recognising and reflecting feelings; trusting the child's ability to solve problems; letting the child lead; not rushing; and setting only the limits that are needed.

**Playroom:**
- Around 12 × 15 feet, or a portable kit.
- **Toy groups:** nurturing (dolls, kitchen items), aggressive-release (soft bats, toy animals with teeth), real-life (family figures, vehicles, a play house), expressive (paint, clay, puppets), mastery (building blocks, games of skill).
- Include toys and figures that reflect the child's culture.

**Core therapist responses (examples written for YourCounselor AI):**

| Skill | Example |
|---|---|
| Tracking what the child does | "You're putting all the animals inside the fence." |
| Reflecting content | "You wanted your cousin to come, and then he couldn't." |
| Reflecting feeling | "You're really cross that the tower keeps falling!" |
| Returning responsibility | "That's something you can decide in here." |
| Encouraging creativity | "You've found your own way to do that." |
| Building self-esteem (praise effort) | "You kept trying until you worked it out." |
| Relationship responses | "You wanted to make sure I got a turn too." |
| **Limit setting (A-C-T, Landreth)** | **A**cknowledge the feeling ("You'd really like to paint on the wall") → **C**ommunicate the limit ("but the wall isn't for painting") → **T**arget an alternative ("You can paint on this big sheet of paper") |

**Formats:**
- **Group CCPT:** 2–3 children (no more than 5), within about a year of age, who want to be accepted by peers; screen out severe aggression or sexualised behaviour.
- **Short-term CCPT:** up to 12 sessions.
- **Intensive CCPT:** more than one session a week.

**Filial therapy / Child-Parent Relationship Therapy (CPRT, Landreth & Bratton):** parents learn play-therapy skills in about 10 weekly 2-hour group sessions and hold supervised 30-minute "special play times" with their child at home each week. Strong evidence (dozens of controlled studies); the approach of choice for children under about 3. A teacher version (Child-Teacher Relationship Training, CTRT) also exists.

### INT-14 CBT for children and adolescents
- **Main strands:** coping skills, cognitive restructuring and problem solving — most modern programmes combine them.
- **Stance:** collaborative empiricism (testing ideas together). Listen for absolutes such as "always", "never" and "should".

**Session template (for the app):**
1. Agree agenda
2. Review homework, including what got in the way
3. Set today's goal
4. Teach and practise a new skill (explanation, role-play)
5. Feedback in both directions
6. Agree homework together
7. Across sessions: help skills transfer to daily life and plan relapse prevention

**Core technique cards:**
- Socratic questioning
- Problem list and step-by-step problem-solving sheet
- Thought record: three columns (what happened · what I thought · what I felt), then a fourth for a more helpful thought
- Self-monitoring charts
- Self-regulation: count helpful and unhelpful behaviours against a goal, then self-reward
- Emotion education and mindfulness: build a wide feelings vocabulary; treat emotions as signals; learn that feelings don't have to be acted on
- Relaxation: slow breathing with a cue word, or PMR. ⚠ Relaxation can raise anxiety in traumatised children, so introduce it as an experiment
- Modelling and role-play
- Exposure with SUDS: imagined, then real; stay until distress drops to the agreed level

**Adapting for under-8s:**
- Fewer talk-based techniques
- Parents as coaches
- Delivery through play
- Active, hands-on learning
- Simple coping phrases instead of disputing

**Protocols for specific problems (summaries):**

| Problem | Main components | Dose / evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Depression (children)** | Learning to tell thoughts from feelings (stories, role-play) → mood rating 1–5 → linking mood with activities → scheduling enjoyable activities → social and assertiveness skills → working on negative views of self, world and future → realistic standards → **praising oneself rather than self-criticism** → relaxation | Based on Stark's school-based programmes; about 12 sessions over 5 weeks. B |
| **Depression (adolescents)** | Psycho-education (with family) · replacing self-defeating thinking · enjoyable activities · social and communication skills · resolving conflict · self-monitoring and goals. Watch for other disorders, hopelessness, self-harm and poor attendance | A (alongside IPT-A) |
| **Anxiety (school-age; Kendall's *Coping Cat* programme)** | Five components: psycho-education → managing body signs of anxiety → changing anxious self-talk → graded exposure → relapse prevention. The programme teaches a four-step coping plan (known by the acronym FEAR) that walks the child through noticing anxious feelings, spotting worried expectations, choosing helpful thoughts and actions, and rewarding their effort. First half builds skills; second half is exposure practice | 14–18 sessions of about 60 minutes over 12–16 weeks. A. Use the licensed manual for delivery |
| **Anxiety (ages 3–8)** | About 40 minutes with the child (reframing worries, answering back to the worry, brave self-talk, one weekly goal) plus 20 minutes with the parent | About 12 weeks. B |
| **OCD** | Psycho-education (OCD as a brain-based condition, reducing blame) → naming obsessions as "OCD talking" → a fear thermometer → **exposure and response prevention** (rate SUDS every 30–60 seconds; graph progress) → taking the power out of obsessive thoughts (e.g., writing them down, reading them aloud, turning them into silly songs) → family stops joining in with rituals → rewards | About 12 sessions. A; family-based versions for ages 5–8 show high remission rates (e.g., POTS Jr trial) |
| **Social anxiety** | Social skills (eye contact → conversations → friendship skills such as sharing, inviting and complimenting) → a three-step social problem-solving routine: *Spot it* (stop and name the problem) · *Think it through* (calm down, list options, predict outcomes) · *Try it* (act, review, praise yourself) → encouraging self-talk for younger children, evidence-checking for older → graded exposure → practice games → booster sessions at 3 and 6 months | A–B |
| **School refusal** | A graded school-return ladder of about 10 steps → coping self-statements → real-life exposure homework → parent involvement. Screen for bullying, separation anxiety, depression, and learning or medical problems | B |
| **Trauma / PTSD (TF-CBT; Cohen, Mannarino & Deblinger)** | Components summarised by the acronym PRACTICE: psycho-education and parenting skills · relaxation · affect regulation · cognitive coping · trauma narrative (spoken, drawn, written or recorded) · in-vivo exposure to safe reminders · conjoint child–parent sessions · enhancing future safety. Non-offending caregivers take part in parallel. ⚠ Do not push a trauma narrative if it isn't helping this particular child | About 12–16 sessions, ages 3–18. **A**. Formal training/certification recommended |
| **Aggression / conduct problems** | Retraining social information processing: notice non-hostile cues → gather more information → don't assume hostile intent → choose friendly goals → think of many possible responses → predict consequences. **Problem-solving steps:** notice there's a problem → pause and think → work out what the conflict is about → decide what you want → brainstorm options → consider consequences → choose and act → review. Add role-play and parent involvement (cf. Lochman's Coping Power) | About 12 weekly group sessions. Moderate effects. B |

**Family CBT (adds to individual CBT):**
- Psycho-education
- Helping parents rethink unhelpful views of their child
- Communication and conflict-resolution skills
- Contingency management
- Planned ignoring of anxious behaviour
- Offering choices rather than deciding for the child
- Allowing the child to struggle and learn
- Teaching self-help skills
- Parents examining how they model anxiety themselves

**Parent track in trauma work:** the parent develops their own account of the trauma → works on blaming themselves or the child → learns stress management → keeps ordinary, age-appropriate discipline (rather than treating the child as "damaged").

**Groups:** advantages — real-world practice, learning from peers, peer feedback. Disadvantages — less individual attention; group dynamics to manage.

**Digital CBT:** computerised, interactive CBT modules (typically 6–8 sessions of 30–45 minutes) can reduce mild to moderate anxiety and depression in 11–16-year-olds. Evidence B. **Directly relevant to app design** — see Appendix D.

### INT-15 REBT with children and adolescents
- **Three core insights (Ellis):**
  1. It is mainly our beliefs about events, not the events themselves, that upset us.
  2. We keep those beliefs alive by repeating them to ourselves.
  3. Changing them takes repeated, deliberate practice.
- **Central irrational process:** demandingness, which leads to three others — catastrophising ("it's awful"), low frustration tolerance ("I can't stand it") and global rating of self or others ("I'm worthless").
- **Common themes:** approval and belonging, achievement, comfort, fairness. In the app, beliefs can be tagged by process × theme.

**REBT session note (template):**
1. Problem
2. Agree goals (emotional change comes first)
3. Assess the emotional and behavioural reaction (C)
4. Assess the triggering event (A)
5. Check for "problems about problems" (e.g., embarrassment about being anxious)
6. Teach that beliefs link events to reactions
7. Identify the irrational beliefs — by following the chain of meaning ("And if that happened, what would that mean to you?") or by offering a tentative guess
8. Connect irrational beliefs with unhealthy emotions and rational beliefs with healthy ones
9. Dispute — using logical, evidence-based and usefulness arguments, in a teaching, Socratic, story, metaphor or humorous style; build a rational alternative; add rational-emotive imagery
10. Strengthen conviction in the new belief
11. Agree practice tasks (call them "practice", not "homework")
12. Review practice
13. Apply the approach to other examples so it generalises

**By age:**
- **Under 8:** no disputing. Use simple coping self-instructions (after Meichenbaum) and problem solving.
- **8–11:** concrete activities, stories and cartoons featuring characters who think rationally; parents join at the start and end of sessions.
- **Adolescents:** Socratic rather than lecturing; **usefulness arguments work best** ("Is thinking this way getting you what you want?"); draw on ideas that peers or popular culture endorse.

**Motivation check before disputing (adapted from DiGiuseppe's work on motivating clients):**
1. Does the young person see the current emotion as a problem for them?
2. Is there an alternative emotional response that would be acceptable in their family and culture?
3. Would working towards it be worthwhile for them?
4. Do they accept that beliefs shape emotions?
5. If yes to all, they are ready to work on beliefs.

**Other points:**
- **Healthy vs unhealthy negative emotions:** concern vs anxiety · sadness vs depression · annoyance vs rage · remorse vs guilt. The aim is a *healthy negative* emotion, not "feeling fine".
- **Belief patterns often seen in children, and what they lead to:**
  - Needing everyone's approval → withdrawal, clinging, attention-seeking
  - Perfectionism → over-working, anxiety, self-criticism
  - Low frustration tolerance → putting things off, defiance
  - Demanding fair treatment from others → anger, revenge, bullying
- **Groups:** work best for social-anxiety problems. Avoid groups of disruptive children unless there is a clear reinforcement system.

**Evidence:** B (moderate effects in children; strongest in pre-adolescents).

### INT-16 Reality therapy / Choice theory (Glasser; Wubbolding)
- **Premise:** all behaviour is chosen "total behaviour" — acting, thinking, feeling and body processes together — aimed at meeting five basic needs: **survival, love and belonging, power (worth and achievement), freedom and fun.**
- **Evidence:** C (few controlled studies). Widely used in schools.
- **Key question:** "Is what you're doing right now getting you what you want?"

**Process steps:**
1. Build a warm, involved relationship
2. Focus on what the young person is doing now
3. Invite them to judge whether it is working
4. Make a plan for responsible behaviour — simple, specific, small, immediate, and within their control
5. Get a commitment (in writing for younger children)
6. Don't accept excuses, but don't criticise either — simply re-plan
7. **No punishment: use logical consequences and making amends**
8. Don't give up on the child

**WDEP framework (Wubbolding):** **W**ants → **D**oing and direction → **E**valuation → **P**lanning.

**Techniques:**
- Humour
- Here-and-now challenge (no excuses)
- **Written contracts** (e.g., "Riya will ask one classmate to join her at lunch by Thursday")
- Teaching skills
- Giving information
- Role-play with feedback
- Support (ask the child's opinion, praise completed plans, express confidence)
- Homework
- Therapeutic reading (always discussed afterwards)
- Brief self-disclosure
- The child sums up the session
- **Restitution** (repair the damage, pay back, or do something kind for the person affected)
- Paradoxical techniques (only for strong resistance)

**Uses in schools:**
- **Class meetings:** for solving group problems, discussing open questions, or reviewing learning.
- **Graduated discipline sequence (after Glasser's school discipline approach):** the teacher first reviews their own approach and makes a fresh start → gives calm, clear directions → asks the student what they are doing, whether it breaks a rule and what they should be doing → helps the student make a plan → if needed, uses escalating options (a time-out area in class, a separate supervised room, being sent home for the day, alternative placement).

**Punishment vs discipline:** punishment is harsh, arbitrary and looks backward. Discipline is logical, taught and looks forward.

### INT-17 Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (SFBT; de Shazer & Berg)
- **Aims:** a cooperative relationship; concrete goals built from the client's "best hopes"; making use of the client's existing strengths.
- **Evidence:** B (growing evidence in schools).
- **Stance:** "leading from one step behind" — the client is the expert on their life. What looks like resistance usually means the pace is too fast or the approach doesn't fit.

**Question bank (wording written for YourCounselor AI):**

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| What has worked before | "What have you already tried? Which bit helped most? What would it take to do more of it?" · "Was there anything a previous counsellor did that was useful?" |
| The client's own ideas about change | "What would need to happen for things to feel better?" · "What would your teacher say needs to change?" |
| Open | "How will you know coming here has been worth it?" |
| Relationship | "Who do you look up to? What would they do in your place?" · "How will your friends notice things are better?" |
| Change before and between sessions | "What's already been different since you booked this appointment?" · "What's gone even a little better since last time?" |
| **Miracle question** | "Imagine that tonight, while you're asleep, something happens and the problem you came with is sorted out — but you don't know it happened. When you wake up, what's the first small thing that tells you life is different? Who else would notice?" Child versions: a magic wand, a wishing star, or "the next episode of your life, without the problem" |
| **Scaling** | "From 0 to 10, where are things today? What would one step up look like?" For young children use a ladder, blocks or stickers ("What could you do this week to climb one step?"). Also rate how useful the session was |
| **Exceptions** | "When is the problem not there, or a bit smaller? In which lesson do you get on best? What's different about it?" · "Which part of the miracle is happening already, even a little?" |
| Coping | "How have you stopped things getting even worse? What keeps you going?" |

**Goal rules:**
- Describe what will be *present*, not just what will be absent ("I'll hand in my homework", not "No more detentions").
- Keep goals small.
- Make them observable ("What would we see if we filmed it?").
- Keep them within the client's control.

**Build on exceptions:** do more of what already works, presented as an experiment (e.g., bring the habits that help in art class into maths).

**Compliments:** sincere and frequent; associated with better outcomes. E.g., "It says a lot that you came today even though you weren't sure about it."

**End-of-session message** (after a 5–10-minute pause):
1. **Compliment**
2. **Bridge** (a link to the client's goals)
3. **Suggestion:** either to notice ("watch for times when things go a bit better") or to do ("try greeting your father when he gets home and notice what happens")

**Applications:** groups (e.g., exam anxiety in teenagers), whole classrooms (e.g., the WOWW approach — Working On What Works), consultation with parents and teachers, and parent–school meetings.

### INT-18 Interpersonal Psychotherapy for Adolescents (IPT-A; Mufson and colleagues) — culturally responsive
- **Evidence:** **A** for adolescent depression.
- **Format:** 12 sessions in three phases of about four sessions each. Culturally adapted versions split sessions into about 30 minutes with the young person and 20 minutes with family.
- **Focus:** depression understood within relationships. Four problem areas: grief, role disputes, role transitions and interpersonal deficits.

**Phase I (assessment and agreement):**
- Explain depression; a limited, temporary "sick role"
- **Interpersonal inventory** of important relationships
- Agree the parents' role
- **Cultural additions:**
  - Assess acculturation using Berry's four strategies: *assimilation · separation · marginalisation · integration*. Integration is typically linked with the least stress, marginalisation and separation with the most.
  - Assess stage of racial/cultural identity development (e.g., Atkinson, Morten & Sue): conformity → dissonance → resistance and immersion → introspection → integrative awareness.
  - Culturally curious questions, e.g., "How would you describe your background?" · "Which people or groups do you feel you belong to?" · "What part does faith play in your family's everyday life?" · "What is expected of boys and girls in your family?"
- Agree a main and a secondary focus, informed by the acculturation pattern — e.g., an adolescent who has moved far from family traditions may be in conflict with parents; one who stays close to them may feel out of place among peers; one who belongs nowhere may be isolated and unsure of identity; one who moves between cultures may struggle when the two worlds clash.

**Phase II (working on the problem area):**
- Define the problem area clearly.
- Link feelings to relationship events.
- **Communication analysis:** listen for culturally loaded statements, e.g., "Nothing works out for people from where I'm from" (discrimination) · "My parents think I've been spoilt by city life" (family explanation of the problem) · "I don't really know who I am" (ethnic, sexual or gender identity) · "I'm one person at home and another at college" (switching between cultures) · "Nobody gets how I talk" (language) · "I've learned not to ask for much" (poverty) · comments about skin colour (colourism).
- Family sessions to negotiate expectations.
- **Role-play and role reversal:** the adolescent plays the parent while the therapist models a new way of communicating.
- Address ethnic, sexual and gender identity, with particular attention to the safety of LGBTQ+ young people (bullying, family rejection, suicide risk).

**Phase III (ending, 2–4 sessions):**
- Talk through feelings about ending.
- Recognise gains, including small ones and fewer negative interactions.
- **Have the adolescent name and explain each skill they've learned.**
- Rehearse future challenges.
- Plan maintenance (e.g., monthly check-ins for a year).

### INT-19 Family and systemic interventions
- **Evidence:** varies by model, B to A (e.g., structural and strategic family therapy for conduct and substance problems; family-based treatment for adolescent eating disorders).

**Structural (Minuchin):** looks at subsystems, boundaries (from over-involved to disengaged) and hierarchy — observe patterns, name them, then change them.
- **Joining:** fit in with each family member, and keep doing so.
- **Mapping:** form hypotheses about power, alliances and boundaries, then test them by watching.
- **Enactment:** ask the family to show the problem in the room, then try a different interaction (e.g., a father and son who usually argue across the room sit next to each other and plan the weekend together).

**Strategic (Haley; MRI brief therapy):** targets the repeating sequences that keep the symptom going, including the family's own "solutions" that backfire.
- Precise questions: who does what, and when?
- A clear, agreed goal.
- **Direct tasks** as homework.
- **Paradoxical instructions** (e.g., "go slowly; don't change yet"): ⚠ L3 only, with a genuine rationale, never as routine.
- **Pretend techniques (Madanes):** the child pretends to have the symptom and the parents pretend to help. Playful and well suited to children.

**Meaning-focused (Milan):**
- **Reframing / positive connotation:** tailor it to this family — never a stock phrase.
- **Circular questions:** "When Aarav refuses to go to school, who is most worried? Who feels most at a loss?"
- **Family rituals:** specific, prescribed and positive (unlike ordeals).

**Solution-focused family work:** miracle question, exceptions, scaling ("What makes it a 4 and not a 3?"), doing more of what works.

**Narrative (White & Epston):**
- **Externalise** the problem, giving it a name the family chooses (e.g., "the Temper", "the Worry Monster").
- Questions about the problem's influence on each person.
- Questions about the family's influence over the problem.
- Metaphors that fit (e.g., "taking back the house from the Chaos").
- Name wider social forces (discrimination, poverty, sexism).
- **Therapeutic letters** and letter-writing campaigns; connect families with others who have overcome similar problems.

**Directive family play therapy (Gil):**
- **Family puppet interview:** the family creates a story with a beginning, middle and end in about 15 minutes, performs it, and the therapist talks with the characters.
- Family drawings taking turns (e.g., "a typical morning at home").
- Sand tray, clay, doll's house.

**Internal Family Systems (IFS; Schwartz):**
- The mind as a system of parts (managers, firefighters, exiles) led by a calm, compassionate core Self.
- Children (from about the concrete-operational stage): represent parts with puppets, drawings or sand-tray figures.
- Adolescents: journaling and art, building understanding for all their parts.

**Medical family therapy:** for chronic illness (e.g., diabetes, asthma, cancer). Coordinate with the medical team; address family roles, treatment adherence, returning to school, and what the illness means to the family.

**Family life-cycle lens:** symptoms often show up at transitions — a new baby, starting school, adolescence, separation, a child leaving home, a death.

### INT-20 Parent and teacher interventions (cross-cutting)
- **Parent training** (e.g., Parent–Child Interaction Therapy, PCIT — Eyberg; behavioural parent training). Evidence A for disruptive behaviour, including in children with intellectual disability. Key parts:
  - Child-led play time in which the parent praises, reflects, imitates and describes the child's play and shows enjoyment (PCIT's "PRIDE" skills)
  - Clear, simple instructions
  - Consistent consequences
  - Time-out
- **Filial therapy / CPRT** (INT-13).
- **Reality-therapy parent involvement:** building involvement, problem-solving steps, communication, discipline rather than punishment.
- **Teacher consultation:**
  - Define the target behaviour precisely
  - Collect ABC data
  - Classroom plan (seating, high ratio of praise to correction, token system, daily report card)
  - Solution-focused classroom work (WOWW)
  - Class meetings
  - Social-emotional learning (SEL) programmes
- **Coordinate with school support services** (IEP or special-education plan) and outside therapists, with signed consent.

## PART 18 — CHILDREN AND ADOLESCENTS WITH DISABILITIES (CHD-30s)

### CHD-30 General adaptations
- Work alongside special-education staff and the child's IEP.
- Consult health, speech and language, occupational therapy and education colleagues.
- Involve the family — parenting stress is often high, and parents make good coaches.
- **Adjust how therapy is delivered:**
  - Simplify materials to match cognitive level.
  - Shorten sessions if the child tires.
  - Use augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) where speech is limited.
  - Meet *more often* when memory problems make learning harder to hold on to.
  - Use visuals throughout.
- Not following through may reflect disorganisation rather than resistance.

### CHD-31 Autism spectrum disorder (ASD)
- **Assessment:** several methods and informants; FBA for behaviour, including what emotional and behavioural difficulties may be communicating.
- **Early intervention:** targets core features, is grounded in theory, is intensive (often 20+ hours a week) and plans ahead for transitions.
- **Therapy structure:** more directive; explicit goals; a **visual schedule**; a goal and a reward for each session; clear links between therapy goals and long-term outcomes.
- **Four-step skill teaching:**
  1. Introduce the skill and why it matters (e.g., a Social Story™ — Gray)
  2. Show it: sort picture cards into correct and incorrect steps
  3. Role-play with feedback
  4. Practise at home, with materials sent to parents
- **Tools:**
  - Social Stories™ (Gray)
  - Comic-strip conversations (Gray)
  - Scripts for predictable situations
  - **Video self-modelling**
  - **Power Cards** (Gagnon — motivating the child through a special interest)
  - Self-management and self-monitoring
  - PMR
  - **Cognitive picture rehearsal** (a picture sequence: trigger → expected behaviour → coping step → reward)
- **Co-occurring anxiety and depression:** modified CBT with longer emotion education and a visual **feelings thermometer**. Evidence B.
- *Note: "Social Stories" is a trademark of Carol Gray; refer to it by name, but write your own stories.*

### CHD-32 Intellectual disability (ID)
- **Diagnosis:** see Core 1 INS-05. ⚠ **Diagnostic overshadowing:** don't put emotional or behavioural difficulties down to the ID itself — co-occurring conditions are common.
- **Anxiety** is frequent (estimates around one in five; specific phobias especially common).
- **What helps:**
  - **Behavioural treatment:** exposure plus reinforcement for phobias is well established.
  - **PCIT** for young children with ID and oppositional behaviour.
  - **Adapted CBT** for emotional difficulties. Many clients can connect feelings with thoughts but find it harder to connect them with behaviour, so keep the work concrete.
  - **SFBT** with simple, concrete scaling.
  - Positive behaviour support plans (aversive methods work no better).
- Track progress with repeated measures; be cautious about medication.

### CHD-33 Specific learning disability (SLD)
- **Associated risks:** low self-image, anxiety, depression, peer difficulties, attention problems — greater with more than one learning disability.
- **Interventions:**
  - Group counselling (social skills, self-advocacy)
  - SFBT
  - Social skills training
  - Parent training
  - Attribution retraining (success comes from effort plus the right strategy)
  - Study skills and self-regulated learning
  - Coordination with remedial teaching
  - India: accommodations under the RPwD Act, 2016 (extra time, a scribe)
- Monitor progress.

### CHD-34 Traumatic brain injury (TBI)
- **Course:** the initial injury, possible secondary damage, then highly variable outcomes. Watch for difficulties with attention, memory, planning and organisation, processing speed, irritability, disinhibition and fatigue, and for personality change.
- **Phases:** hospital → **planned return to school** (reduced timetable, rest breaks, a named staff contact, helping classmates understand) → long-term monitoring. New difficulties may appear as developmental demands grow.
- **Interventions:**
  - Behavioural approaches (managing triggers; positive behaviour support)
  - Family therapy and parent support (grief, changed roles)
  - Social skills and peer-support programmes
  - Referral for cognitive rehabilitation (L3 / neuropsychology)

### CHD-35 Physical disability and chronic illness
- **Common issues:**
  - Pain
  - Missed school
  - Body image
  - Balancing dependence and independence
  - Being left out by peers
  - Sticking to treatment
  - Frightening hospital experiences
  - Parental over-protection and stress
  - Siblings' needs
- **Interventions:**
  - Behavioural (reinforcing adherence and activity; graded activity)
  - Cognitive (pain coping — distraction, imagery, relaxation, coping self-talk, with parents as coaches)
  - Family work (medical family therapy)
  - Liaison with school

## PART 19 — CULTURALLY RESPONSIVE PRACTICE WITH YOUNG PEOPLE (CHD-20s)

### CHD-20 Core ideas
- **Culture:** the shared ideas, symbols, values and beliefs connected with ethnicity, caste, class, language, religion, disability, sexual orientation and more.
- **Collectivism:** valuing the group ("we") above the individual ("me"), often with family honour at stake. It can protect (support, belonging) or add strain (conflict, isolation).
- **The therapeutic relationship includes** the child, the family and their culture.

**Therapist cultural competence — three parts:** awareness of your own cultural lens and biases · cultural knowledge, *while staying alert to differences within any group* (knowledge is not stereotyping) · the ability to build solutions from the client's own perspective and context.

### CHD-21 Assessment additions
- **Acculturation strategy** (Berry) of the young person and of each parent; gaps between them often drive family conflict.
- **Stage of ethnic or cultural identity development.**
- **Sexual and gender identity:** safety, family acceptance, bullying, suicide risk.
- **The family's explanation of the problem:** e.g., spiritual causes, evil eye, a phase, family shame.
- **Help-seeking patterns:** family first, religious or traditional healers, presenting physical symptoms first.
- **Economic stress and experiences of discrimination.** Perceived discrimination affects wellbeing whether or not it can be proven, so work with its *psychological impact*.
- **Language:** use a trained interpreter — never a sibling or relative; get consent for the interpreter; note any effect on test validity.

### CHD-22 Group-specific strengths and challenges
*Most published research on this topic comes from the US. The US summary is kept for reference; the India section is the priority for this app.*

| Group (US research) | Frequently noted strengths | Frequently noted challenges |
|---|---|---|
| African American | Extended kinship networks; faith communities; preparing children to handle racism | Justified mistrust rooted in racism; need for discrimination to be acknowledged |
| American Indian / Alaska Native | Tribal and elder connections; spirituality and connection to land; holistic view of wellbeing | Historical and intergenerational trauma; conflict over cultural change |
| Asian American | Strong family ties; respect for elders | Distress shown physically; stigma; hierarchy; generational gaps in acculturation; racism |
| Latino/a | Close family bonds; emphasis on warm personal relationships; faith | Generational acculturation conflict; stigma; discrimination |
| Arab / Muslim American | Community solidarity; faith as a source of coping | Reluctance to seek outside help; discrimination; generational conflict |

**India (primary for this app):**
- **Strengths:** joint and extended family, faith and spirituality, community networks, respect for elders.
- **Challenges:**
  - Academic pressure and competitive exams
  - Caste and class discrimination
  - Conflict over gender roles
  - Migration from villages to cities
  - Stigma about mental health ("log kya kahenge")
  - Distress described in bodily or everyday terms (e.g., "tension", "dimaag kharab", stomach "gas")
  - Seeking help from faith healers first
  - Many languages
  - Family rejection of LGBTQ+ young people
  - Parents deciding careers and marriages
- Favour family-inclusive, respectful approaches with plenty of psycho-education.
